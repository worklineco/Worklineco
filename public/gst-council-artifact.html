Background: the journey of the issue
  1. 2017GST launched

    The refund formula counts only credit on inputs. Input services and capital goods are left out.

  2. 2020High Courts split

    Gujarat High Court reads input services into the formula. Madras High Court upholds the restriction.

  3. Sep 2021Supreme Court rules

    The restriction is upheld in VKC Footsteps. The Court urges the Council to reconsider the formula.

  4. Sep 2025GST 2.0 widens the gap

    Many goods move to 5% while services stay at 18%.

  5. Oct 202657th Council opens refund

    Refund of ITC availed on input services from 1 November 2026, and on capital goods from 1 April 2027.

Bottom lineInput-service credit stops piling up from 1 November 2026. Credit already accumulated stays under the existing formula. The gain is largest where services, not raw material, make up most of the cost:
  • Mining of minor minerals and quarrying. Sand, stone chips, limestone, and marble and granite blocks sell at 5%, against mining contracts, royalty under reverse charge, equipment hire and transport at 18%. Earthmovers and crushers add a capital goods refund on ITC availed on or after 1 April 2027. Metal ores and coal already moved to 18%, so the relief is mainly for quarry and minor mineral operators.
  • FMCG, packaged food and pharmaceuticals. Many products and most medicines are at 5%, while packaging, advertising, cold storage, logistics, testing, clinical research and IT services carry 18%.
  • Textiles, garments and footwear. The product goes out at 5%, while design, rent, logistics and marketing come in at 18%.
  • Renewable energy, fertilisers and farm machinery. Solar modules, wind turbines, fertilisers, tractors and farm machinery sell at 5%, against engineering, erection, EPC, handling, transport and plant maintenance services at 18%.
Track input-service credit separately from 1 November 2026, and plan major equipment purchases around 1 April 2027.

Exports and SEZ: zero-rated refunds

ReliefAct and rules

Capital goods ITC becomes refundable on exports, and the 1.5 times cap on export value is removed.

  • Refund of accumulated credit on capital goods allowed for zero-rated supplies, spread over 60 months.
  • Rule 89(4)(C) amended to remove the restriction of zero-rated turnover of goods to 1.5 times the value of like goods supplied domestically.
  • FORM RFD-01 to capture details in system-readable form, dispensing with scanned uploads for zero-rated and inverted duty claims.

Background
  • For exports under a letter of undertaking, the refund formula in rule 89(4) counts "net ITC" as credit on inputs and input services only. Credit on capital goods has never been refundable on this route, so export units with heavy machinery have often absorbed that tax as cost, unless they had domestic tax to set it off against.
  • Rule 89(4)(C) capped export value at 1.5 times the domestic price of like goods. Meant to stop over-invoicing, it cut refunds of genuine exporters with higher export prices or no domestic sales.
Bottom lineFor exporters working under a letter of undertaking, GST paid on machinery and equipment has so far not been refundable. Capital goods ITC availed on or after 1 April 2027 becomes refundable, at 1/60 a month over 60 months. The businesses that gain most:
  • IT and software companies delivering offshore. Servers, network equipment, laptops and power backup for development and support centres serving foreign clients carry 18%. Nearly all service exporters work under LUT, so this credit tends to accumulate.
  • Global capability centres, BPO and KPO units, and data centres serving overseas customers. Servers, cooling and power systems make up most of the cost, and hardware is refreshed every three to four years, so the refund becomes a large and recurring cash flow rather than a one-time gain.
  • Export manufacturers. Pharma and API plants, engineering and auto component makers running CNC machines, and textile and garment units with looms and stitching lines.

Background
  • Section 115 allows interest on refund of pre-deposit from the date of payment, but borrows the rate from section 56, which carries two rates: 6% where a refund is not paid within 60 days of the application, and 9% where a refund arising from an order of an appellate authority, the Tribunal or a court is not paid within 60 days of the application filed after that order.

IGST refund on exports, rule 96(10)

ReliefRules, retrospective

Exporters who imported under advance authorisation keep their IGST refunds, back to 2017.

  • Omission of rule 96(10) to be made retrospective, in line with the Supreme Court's decision.
EffectiveRetrospectively from 23 October 2017
Background
  • Rule 96(10) denied refund of IGST paid on exports where the exporter had imported inputs under concessional schemes such as advance authorisation or EOU.
  • It was omitted by Notification 20/2024-Central Tax from 8 October 2024, without a saving clause. The department continued to pursue recovery for earlier periods.
  • In Goodluck India Ltd v. Union of India (SLP (C) 24550 of 2025, decided 6 August 2026), the Supreme Court held that the omission applies to all proceedings pending on that date, and dismissed the Revenue's appeals. It relied on the Constitution Bench in Kolhapur Canesugar Works Ltd v. Union of India (2000) 2 SCC 536, which holds that a rule omitted without a saving clause cannot sustain pending proceedings. CBIC accepted the ruling by Office Memorandum dated 24 August 2026.
  • The Council's recommendation goes a step further: by making the omission effective from 23 October 2017, the rule falls away for the whole period, not only for matters pending on 8 October 2024.
Bottom lineDemands to recover IGST refunds under rule 96(10) lose their basis for the entire period. In pending matters, seek closure now on the strength of Goodluck India. Where recovery was completed before 8 October 2024, examine a refund claim once the retrospective notification issues.

Blocked credits, section 17(5)

ReliefAct amendment

Six heads of blocked credit are opened.

  • Outdoor catering.
  • Health and life insurance.
  • Telecommunication towers.
  • Pipelines laid outside factory premises.
  • Free samples.
  • Goods destroyed or written off on expiry of shelf life as required by law.
Background
  • Outdoor catering. Section 17(5)(b) allowed credit only where the employer was legally obliged to provide it, or where it was used for supplying catering.
  • Health and life insurance. Section 17(5)(b) allowed credit only where the employer was legally obliged to provide such cover to employees.
  • Towers and pipelines. The explanation to section 17 kept both out of "plant and machinery", so they were blocked as immovable property. Under the earlier CENVAT regime, the Supreme Court in Bharti Airtel Ltd v. Commissioner of Central Excise, Pune (20 November 2024) held mobile towers and prefabricated shelters to be movable and allowed credit, but the express exclusion under GST kept towers blocked. The Supreme Court's functionality test in Chief Commissioner of CGST v. Safari Retreats Pvt Ltd (2024) raised hopes, but the Finance Act, 2025 amended section 17(5)(d) retrospectively from 1 July 2017 and closed that route.
  • Free samples and expired stock. Section 17(5)(h) required reversal of credit on samples and on goods destroyed or written off. Pharma and FMCG companies paid twice: they lost the stock and also had to reverse the credit, even where food and drug laws required the destruction.
Bottom lineEveryday business costs now earn credit, which lowers the cost of running the business.

Section 16(2)(c): protection for honest buyers

ReliefReferred to committee

A genuine buyer's input tax credit should not be denied because of a supplier's default — referred to a committee of officers, to take effect from 1 April 2027.

  • The agenda was to amend section 16(2)(c), under which a genuine buyer's ITC was blocked or reversed whenever any supplier in the value chain failed to deposit the tax or passed on fake credit.
  • Credit is denied even where the buyer holds the invoice, has received the goods or services and has paid the supplier — only because someone further down the chain is non-compliant.
  • The Council agreed the position needs to be rationalised, balancing relief for honest buyers with curbing misuse of ITC.
  • On the request of several states and in the spirit of cooperative federalism, the matter was referred to a standing committee of officers for examination.
  • The committee is to report within three months; the Council will then take a final decision, for implementation from 1 April 2027.
Bottom lineAn honest buyer who holds valid documents and has paid the supplier should not lose credit for someone else's default. The exact shape is to be settled by the committee.

Arrest, prosecution, notices and penalties

Watch This Topic6 recommendations
Effective date On issue of the law amendment, rule, notification or circular, unless a specific date is shown on the card.

The character of a GST proceeding changes here. Arrest by GST officers goes, prosecution focuses on fraudulent conduct, and small notices stop.

Power of arrest

ReliefAct amendment

Section 69 goes. The proper officer under GST loses the power to arrest.

  • The Council has recommended complete withdrawal of the proper officer's power of arrest by omission of section 69 of the CGST Act. This is a full deletion, not a restriction to serious cases.
  • What goes is arrest by the tax officer. Prosecution for fraud continues under section 132, through the courts.
  • Summons under section 70, and search and seizure under section 67, are not touched by the release.
Background
  • Section 69 allowed a Commissioner to authorise a proper officer to arrest where the tax evaded or credit wrongly taken exceeded ₹2 crore.
  • Concerns were raised about arrests and payments made during searches. CBIC issued Instructions 01/2022-23 on payments during search and 02/2022-23 on conditions for arrest.
  • In Radhika Agarwal v. Union of India (27 February 2025), the Supreme Court held that arrest needs recorded reasons based on material, that criminal procedure safeguards apply, and that payments made under coercion can be challenged.
Bottom lineTax disputes go back to being tax disputes. A business facing a mismatch, a classification question or a credit dispute will deal with notices and hearings, not the threat of custody. Fraud remains a criminal matter.
Section 69 omitted

Prosecution

ReliefAct amendment

Criminal exposure starts at ₹5 crore and is focused on fraudulent conduct.

₹1 crore→₹5 crore
  • Clause (i) of section 132(1) is omitted. The words "evades tax" go from clause (e), and "or in any other manner deals with" goes from clause (h).
  • Clause (c), on fraudulent credit, is narrowed to credit taken without receipt of goods or services, or without an invoice or bill.
  • Punishment for the various offences under section 132 is to be rationalised.
Background
  • Section 132 scales punishment by amount: up to five years above ₹5 crore, three years between ₹2 crore and ₹5 crore, and one year between ₹1 crore and ₹2 crore.
  • The 48th Council (2022) raised the threshold for launching prosecution to ₹2 crore, except fake invoicing which stayed at ₹1 crore, and decriminalised obstruction, tampering with evidence and failure to supply information.
  • Broad words such as "evades tax" in clause (e) and "deals with" in clause (h) left room to threaten prosecution even in ordinary disputes.
Bottom lineA classification, valuation or interpretation dispute is unlikely to attract prosecution. Fake invoicing stays criminal.

Show cause notices and penalties

ReliefAct amendment

No notice below ₹10,000, and that applies to pending matters.

  • A minimum threshold of ₹10,000 (CGST plus SGST plus IGST plus cess) for issuing a show cause notice under sections 73, 74 and 74A.
  • A statutory provision that notices and appeals below ₹10,000 that are pending when the threshold comes into force will be decided as if the threshold had applied when the notice was issued.
  • Penalty to be deemed a charge where the full tax is paid voluntarily with interest and penalty within the specified time.
  • Reduced penalty of 5% in non-fraud cases where tax and interest are paid within 30 days (section 73) or 60 days (section 74A) of the adjudication order.
  • The minimum penalty of ₹10,000 in non-fraud cases is removed.
  • The maximum general penalty under section 125 falls from ₹25,000 to ₹10,000.
  • A circular on the quality of notices and orders, timely issuance, invocation of fraud only on merits, natural justice and the conduct of personal hearings.
Effective₹10,000 floor applies to notices and appeals pending on the date it comes into force
Bottom lineList every pending notice and appeal below ₹10,000 now. Those drop away by statute. For larger non-fraud demands, the 5% penalty on payment within 30 days changes the arithmetic of settling versus contesting a weak point.

E-way bill and interception

ReliefAct amendment

No interception in transit states, and none without a Joint Commissioner's authorisation.

  • A conveyance can be intercepted only on specific intelligence and with the authorisation of an officer not below Joint Commissioner.
  • Inspection, detention or seizure only where the supplier or the recipient is located or registered in the state of interception. No interception in transit states.
  • Where no e-way bill has been generated, or the conveyance carries no document showing origin or destination, goods can be inspected, detained or seized irrespective of jurisdiction.
  • Confiscation under section 130 will not apply to goods or conveyances in transit.
Bottom lineThe roadside stop in a state you neither ship from nor sell into ends. But a vehicle without an e-way bill or proper documents can still be stopped anywhere, so keep every consignment fully documented.

Bunching of notices

CautionAct amendment

Bunching of notices across financial years to be validated by law.

  • A validation clause in the CGST Act for notices that courts have held invalid on the ground of bunching, that is, a single notice covering more than one financial year.
Background
  • Sections 73 and 74 allow a notice "for any period", but the time limit under sections 73(10) and 74(10) runs separately for each financial year. Departments often issued one notice covering several years.
  • Not permitted: the Madras High Court in Titan Company Ltd v. Joint Commissioner (2024) held that a single notice cannot cover more than one financial year, since limitation runs year by year. Later Madras High Court rulings followed this view.
  • Permitted: the Delhi High Court in Ambika Traders v. Additional Commissioner, DGGSTI (29 July 2025) upheld a consolidated notice under section 74, holding it may even be needed where fraudulent credit runs across years.
  • The validation clause settles this split by law.
Bottom lineThis is the one burden item in the release for litigants. If a pending defence rests on bunching of notices, that ground will fall away once the clause is enacted, and the matter will have to be argued on merits. Limitation must still be checked year by year, and the retrospective validation may itself face challenge.

E-commerce operators, section 9(5)

CautionAct amendment

Platforms pay GST on notified services, whatever their business model.

  • Section 9(5) amended to make the e-commerce operator liable to pay tax on notified services irrespective of the business model it follows.
Background
  • Under section 9(5), the operator pays GST on notified services supplied through its platform, such as cab rides, accommodation, housekeeping and restaurant services, as if it were the supplier.
  • Some platforms argued they were only a technology or listing service, charging drivers or vendors a flat subscription instead of a commission, or letting customers pay the provider directly, and so fell outside section 9(5).
Bottom lineIf a notified service is booked through your platform, you pay the tax, whether you earn by commission or subscription and whether or not you collect the payment. Platforms on subscription or similar models should review their position.

Export of services

ReliefIGST Act amendment

Services through your foreign branch, and work on a foreign client's goods, qualify as export.
